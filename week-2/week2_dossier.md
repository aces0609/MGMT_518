## The six recordings

### 1. Siebel Chapter 3: The Information Age Accelerates (26:25) [1]

- **Convergence:** Cloud, big data, AI and IoT mature together and amplify each other. In a connected car, sensors feed the cloud, data is aggregated, models learn, and results flow back to navigation.
- **Each pillar in a line:** Cloud turns capital expense into operating expense. Big data is now a trust problem (quality, governance, bias). AI creates as well as predicts, but few firms have found value. IoT's value is continuous data (glucose monitors, smart thermostats).
- **Business model first:** A model defines how value is created, delivered and captured (Netflix works; WeWork's long leases against short memberships did not). People adopt technology that helps them hit their function's KPIs.
- **Adoption (as cited):** IT about 62%, marketing and sales about 40%, operations cautious, HR, finance and legal slowest. Generative AI pilots fell from about 25% of companies to about 18% actively using it. McKinsey is cited saying about 30% of hours could be automated by 2030.

### 2. Cyber Security (length not listed) [2]

- **Evolution:** Perimeter defense, then detection and response, then security by design, Zero Trust ("never assume, always verify") and resilience planning. Chris Painter's definition: a continuous cycle of protection, detection, response and recovery.
- **New attack surfaces:** IoT (devices not built to be secure; about 15 billion connected in 2024), cloud (one breach hits thousands of organizations) and AI (trusted and untrusted data blur).
- **Generative AI:** It cannot reliably separate data from instructions, which enables prompt injection. The "lethal trifecta" is untrusted content, private data access and the ability to act; avoid combining all three.
- **Takeaway:** Everything is hackable, most breaches start with people, and external data should be treated as untrusted.

| Case | What happened | Lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Insecam (2014) | Site streamed 8,000+ cameras still on default passwords | Change factory defaults |
| St. Jude Medical (2016) | MedSec skipped responsible disclosure and partnered with a short seller; stock fell about 5% in a day | Ethics of disclosure |
| Stuxnet (2010) | Malware reached Iran's air-gapped Natanz plant via USB and spoofed normal readings | First known cyberattack with physical damage |
| Change Healthcare (2024) | Stolen low-level credentials, no multi-factor authentication, slow detection; $22 million ransom; 193 million people exposed | Breaches start with people and missing basics |

*Cases as told by the instructor [2]. Ransomware is now an industry (attacks up about 25% from 2024 to 2025), and healthcare is targeted because downtime endangers patients [2].*

### 3. The Importance of Customer Insight in Innovation & Transformation (19:19) [3]

- **Numbers game:** Failure rates are high everywhere (cited: 95% of GenAI pilots, about 93% of drugs, about 75% of ERP projects). Odds improve with problem-first thinking, disciplined experiments and customer validation.
- **Segway:** Famous inventor, big budget, about 24,000 units in five years against a plan of 10,000 per week. An invention, not an innovation.
- **Jobs to be done:** The outcome a customer wants in a context; stable over time and solution-agnostic. Three dimensions: functional, emotional, social. Judge a problem by intensity (shark bite or mosquito bite), frequency and density, and whether people will pay.
- **Research tools, cheap to costly:** existing data, surveys, VOC interviews, ethnography, experiments. Surveys miss the "why"; focus groups get dominated by loud voices; "it's easy to open your mouth, hard to open your wallet."

### 4. Problem Definition (12:00) [4]

- **Metric and baseline:** "Make the GenAI chat better" is vague; "cut response time to under 10 seconds" is a target. Beware the HIPPO (Highest Paid Person's Opinion).
- **Five whys:** Keep asking why to reach the root cause (Toyota, 1950s). In the Jefferson Memorial parable, erosion traced back to bird droppings, spiders, insects and finally floodlights switched on too early.
- **Airport case:** A student team found concessions were the lever; interviews showed travelers did not know the airport enforces street pricing. 7 of 11 said knowing would make them buy more, which still needs experiments.

### 5. VOC Recruitment and Interview Plan (7:29) [5]

- **Recruiting:** Define the respondent profile and count first. With a sponsor, send a short email (purpose, time, call to action). Otherwise use social posts or your own network.
- **Screener:** Short, mostly multiple choice, brackets for age and income, a note that answers are used only for class, and an opening question that checks the person does the activity.
- **Tools:** Calendly (scheduling), Zoom (record, with consent), Otter.ai (transcribe), a content grid of quotes by question, Miro or slides to share insights.

### 6. VOC Discussion Guide & Problem Interviews (4:26) [6]

- **Run it:** Open briefly, ask permission to record, ask for stories ("When was the last time you...?"). Stay on the surface until you hear strong emotion, then "tell me more."
- **Avoid:** Speculation about future behavior, pitching, implying the person has a problem, leading yes/no questions. Favor what, when, where, who, how and why.
- **Practical:** A note-taker is fine if the interviewer is fully present. Two sample guides are on Canvas.

## HBR reading: Jobs to Be Done

*Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan, HBR, Sept 2016 [9]. Paraphrased from the full text, with short quotes only.*

**Core idea.** Customers "hire" a product to make progress in a specific circumstance, and "fire" it if it does the job badly. Data built on correlations (age, income, who prefers version A) misses the cause of a purchase. Disruption theory explains how incumbents lose; jobs theory explains how to build what people want.

**Condo example.** A Detroit-area builder targeting downsizers saw traffic but few sales, and features like bay windows did not help. Consultant Bob Moesta had buyers draw timelines of their decision. The pattern was the dining room table: buyers could not move until they decided what to do with something that represented their family. The builder made room for a table and added moving services, storage and a sorting room. It raised prices $3,500, and in 2007, while industry sales fell 49%, it grew 25%.

**Four principles**
1. A job is what someone wants to accomplish in a circumstance, not just a task.
2. Circumstances matter more than customer traits, attributes or trends.
3. Good innovations solve problems with poor or no existing solutions.
4. Jobs always have social and emotional dimensions.

**Beyond the job.** Nielsen found only 92 of 20,000+ new products (2012 to 2016) sold over $50 million in year one and held sales in year two; each solved a specific, poorly done job. Lasting advantage needs the right experience (American Girl) and aligned processes (Southern New Hampshire University's 8.5-minute follow-up goal).

**Five questions to find jobs**
1. Is there a job you need done yourself?
2. Where is there nonconsumption?
3. What workarounds have people invented? (Small businesses using Quicken)
4. What tasks do people want to avoid? (CVS MinuteClinic)
5. What surprising uses have customers found? (NyQuil taken for sleep led to ZzzQuil)

**B2B sidebar.** Intercom's interviews with new and churned customers revealed four jobs, and customers used different words than the company did.

**For your project.** Moesta's timeline method is the same kind of VOC interview as in the lectures [3][6]: ask about the last time someone dealt with the problem, listen for pushes (problem) and pulls back (inertia, anxiety), and note the emotional and social side of the job.

## In class: Kristy Edwards

Her talk argues that AI-era attacks are mostly old threats at greater scale and speed, plus a few new ones such as prompt injection and autonomous agents. Defenders must cover both, and security is everyone's job [11].

**A point of view you could bring** (use only if it matches yours): in regulated or high-stakes settings, the question is rarely whether a team can build something but whether it can get permission to deploy it. Security by design, least privilege and agent guardrails earn that permission.

**Questions**
1. You said agents force us to rethink permissions, identity and data access. What does least privilege look like for an AI agent, and who approves it?
2. For organizations that build decisions on sensor and equipment data, where would you start securing the pipeline, and how should teams treat inbound data given prompt injection?
3. You said security is underfunded because success is invisible. How do you make the business case before an incident?
4. With attackers and defenders both gaining from AI, what should a non-security leader do first in the next 90 days?
