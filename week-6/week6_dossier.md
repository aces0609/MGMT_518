## Lecture: Effective Data Storytelling

*30 minutes [1]. The framework is Brent Dykes's arc; the before/after craft comes from Kurnoff & Lazarus (the captions garble the name as "Koenig and Lazarus").*

- **Two structures for two jobs:** the news article's inverted pyramid (headline first — how to write each individual slide) versus the narrative arc (context → tension → resolution — how to structure the whole deck). "Don't bury the lead" on a slide; don't start the deck with "Harry Potter defeats Voldemort" [1].
- **Dykes's four-part arc:** setting and hook → rising insights → the aha moment (big idea) → resolution and next steps. Build the arc on a whiteboard before opening PowerPoint [1].
- **The campus-gym walkthrough:** from a jumble of stats, the 12% membership drop is the *conflict*, not the aha. The aha is the 62% vs 29% renewal gap between members who take a class or find a gym buddy and those who don't; the resolution is a free class pass + buddy match, framed as a randomized experiment with success criteria set *before* running it, so nobody moves the goalposts [1].
- **The big idea test:** a simple, conversational statement of the "what" plus one or two benefits ("To stop the leaky bucket of membership decline we need to build connection and community"). Perform the "so what" test; spend your time here before building slides [1].
- **Craft tips:** titles are headlines (executives read only the titles); oversized metrics; chunk text into colored shapes; fresh-but-clear chart forms; photos sparing, consistent and accessible. Avoid "Franken-decks." The audience "doesn't want to watch you make the cake" — skip the process slides [1].
- **Decide where your deck sits** on the TED-talk ↔ novel spectrum; a hybrid deck pairs strong headlines and clean visuals with short captions so it survives without you. Flip the camera: it's about the audience, not you [1].

## Lecture: Solution Storming

*12 minutes [2]. The project enters the second diamond: go broad before narrow — "fall in love with the problem, not the solution."*

- **"How Might We" questions** give brainstorming structure: not "how might we make our website better" (too broad) or "make the visuals more entertaining" (too narrow), but "how might we make our website more appealing for segment X" [2].
- **The client story:** a device brainstorm with only sales and marketing produced nothing new. The rerun mixed engineers, designers, material scientists and developers, gave each a specific How-Might-We brief and three days of homework, and produced 60+ ideas refined into ~15-19 concepts [2].
- **Capture ideas as sketches/idea napkins:** a sticky name, a quick description, and why it lives. "Ideas aren't real until they're visible" [2].
- **When stuck:** look into the past (Corning pulled Gorilla Glass from its archives for the iPhone); add constraints (constraints breed creativity); ask "what would Nike do"; and use AI as a brainstorming partner — Jeremy Utley (Stanford d.school) calls AI "a cognitive amplifier" and an escape from "cognitive quicksand" [2].
- **Affinity-map the output** to find thin clusters and cross-cluster combinations before narrowing [2].
- **Don't ask customers for ideas:** "innovators innovate, customers validate." Jobs didn't run focus groups; Apple sent ethnographers to watch people struggle. Ask customers about problems, not solutions [2].

## Lecture: Solution Selection

*19 minutes [3]. Creativity meets discipline: a structured process keeps ego and bias in check (Furr & Dyer: teams pick what's easy, or their own baby — "no one wants to hear their baby's ugly").*

The tools, arranged on the lecture's 2×2 (effort × intuitive-vs-analytical):

| Tool | What it is | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dot voting | Quick, democratic temperature-take | Early alignment, not final decisions |
| Impact-effort matrix | Plot ideas by business impact vs effort | Gut check; the value is the debate |
| Trade-off matrix | Rate ideas against weighted criteria | Make-or-buy decisions, job offers |
| Pugh matrix | Compare each concept better/same/worse against a reference ("datum"); winner becomes the next datum | Avoids false precision; the Siemens case |
| AHP | Pairwise-compare criteria, derive numerical weights | High-stakes, contested choices |
| Sensitivity analysis / Monte Carlo | Stress-test how inputs change the decision / simulate outcome probabilities | When assumptions dominate |

- **The Siemens case** (the captions render it "E.Cam"): co-creation sessions put a nurse, an administrator and a surgeon in the same room to trade off requirements; "creative forbearance" (your first idea isn't your best) and "leapfrogging" (aim 10x, not incremental). The instructor notes personal connections to the case authors (names as captioned: Albrecht Enders, Herb Velasquez, Melissa Appleyard — spellings unverified) [3].
- **The AHP industrial-design story:** instead of a customer vote ("industrial design is not a popularity contest"), criteria were pulled from unprompted VOC quotes, staff did pairwise comparisons, and the math exposed *why* groups disagreed (sales weighted brand continuity; engineering weighted ease of use). The outcome matched the PM's gut — but produced buy-in and a documented, defensible decision [3].
- **Closing line:** great teams don't decide by instinct or hierarchy ("no HiPPOs in the room"); the tools make thinking visible [3].

## Lecture: Solution Validation

*33 minutes [4]. Test the riskiest assumptions first, as cheaply as possible: learn fast, don't fail expensively.*

- **Assumption mapping:** every idea rests on desirability (do they want it?), feasibility (can we build it?) and viability (should we — can we make money, does it fit our business?). Plot assumptions on uncertainty × importance and test the top-right first. Examples: Google bolted sensors and a laptop onto a Prius before Waymo existed (feasibility); a test-and-measurement company's beloved probe glue died because the business sold high-margin hardware, not consumables (viability — captions render the company as "Tesla measurement") [4].
- **The build curve** (Giff Constable): match confidence in customer value to cost of building. Low confidence + high cost = the danger zone; low confidence + low cost = just build it, which is the Starbucks pumpkin-spice-latte story — the focus group said no, the test market cost a syrup and some posters, and it became a ~half-billion-dollar drink (as stated in the lecture). Its counterexample: Chantico, pulled within a year [4].
- **The truth curve:** pick the lowest-effort test that gives believable learning — interviews → paper tests → ad tests → button-to-nowhere → Wizard of Oz (a human behind the curtain faking the product, especially valuable for AI concepts: fake the model before training it) [4].
- **Hypotheses are IF-THEN, specific, measurable, falsifiable:** "If we show a two-minute video of our AI inventory dashboard to 10 cafe owners, at least six express interest and three sign up for a pilot within a week." Look for the black swan — the single observation that disproves you — and demand skin in the game (time, email, money; Tesla's Model 3 deposits are the extreme case). The class will use Strategyzer's experiment card [4].
- **Prototypes answer a specific question** and shouldn't be over-built: the medical-device example deliberately exaggerated the size-vs-battery-life trade-off (and included "none of the above" to measure stickiness of the status quo) [4].
- **Reporting:** problem score vs WOW score per respondent (the "your baby is ugly" chart), segmented by what actually explains choices (patient type, not country or profession) [4].
- **Pivot or persevere:** 90% of initial ideas lack problem-solution fit (Furr & Dyer, as cited). Keep the product and change the customer, or keep the customer and change the problem — Instagram was Burbn, YouTube was a dating site. Nike handed out badges for killing ideas: reward finding out early [4].

## How the pieces connect

- **The four lectures are one pipeline for the Storyline (#4):** your VOC themes become rising insights, your top challenge becomes the conflict, your solution direction becomes the big aha, and a validation experiment (IF-THEN, success criteria up front) is the strongest possible resolution slide [1][4][6].
- **Week 4's visualization rules are assumed:** headline titles, do-the-math-for-the-audience, charts over tables — the storytelling lecture layers the narrative arc on top of them [1].
- **The gym example is a template** for your deck's shape: conflict stat → three rising insights that eliminate the usual suspects → the renewal-gap aha → an experiment as resolution [1].
- **Your Mid-Project themes already map to the tools:** "human access changes the odds" and the silence problem are How-Might-We material [2]; a trade-off or Pugh matrix can rank your three solution directions [3]; and each direction's riskiest assumption is testable with a landing page, demo video or Wizard-of-Oz test before Week 9 [4].
- **Echoes of earlier weeks:** "innovators innovate, customers validate" extends Week 2's VOC discipline; the experiment framing (success criteria before you run it) repeats the Week 4 exploration lecture's warning against starting from conclusions [2][4].
