## Lecture: the organizational imperative

*Instructor lecture [1].*

- **Existential, not optional:** Companies that fold cloud, AI, data and IoT into core strategy survive; the rest fade. Blockbuster (ignored streaming), Yahoo (missed buying Google and Facebook) and Borders (outsourced e-commerce to Amazon) were leadership failures, not technology failures.
- **Data moats:** More data makes the AI smarter, which draws more users and generates more data. Incumbents are not doomed: they have large, high-quality data and capital.
- **Four common threads of success:** a CEO-driven mandate; high-value, measurable use cases; digital factories or centers of excellence; a systematic rollout with measurement.
- **CEO action plan:** Make the executive team the transformation engine, with each member owning part of the digital agenda. Define the future state, find the gaps, publish a roadmap that evolves, and communicate constantly.
- **Pace:** Do not freeze and do not sprint blindly. Use short agile cycles and compound small wins. Rule of thumb from the lecture: if a project does not deliver visible results within a year, do not do it.
- **Culture and learning:** Digital culture is about values (collaboration, learning, risk-taking), not beanbags. Treat failures as data. Leaders need digital fluency without needing to code. Benchmark against leaders; warning signs are high costs, rigid or asset-heavy structures and slow innovation cycles.
- **Three takeaways:** It is a leadership challenge, not an IT project; the CEO aligns strategy, culture and technology; speed, learning and economic value are the measures.
- **Debate set up for class:** Siebel's top-down view versus Nathan Christensen's view that employees must also own part of the change.

## Stephen Sklarew, Synaptiq

*CEO and co-founder of Synaptiq, a Portland AI consultancy [2][3][5].*

**Background [2].** About 28 years in software. Synaptiq grew from a 2015 AI project he started with a former colleague, Dr. Tim Oates. The firm engages at the strategy level first (business problems, data types, tools, process) and then moves to implementation. Examples he gives: document processing for law firms, coral reef restoration farms, and a USAID project in Rwanda.

**Interview [3].**
- **AI adds a layer to digital transformation:** Beyond moving data into systems, you must make the data viable and valuable to fuel AI. Data is "the long tail" of the work, and having data is not the same as having data that is representative and deep enough.
- **Probabilistic software:** Traditional software is deterministic (requirements, code, quality assurance). AI models combine a problem, data and an algorithm and output probabilities, which is why the same question can get different answers. People struggle to trust probabilistic output.
- **Where firms go wrong:** They apply AI everywhere without asking whether the data and the problem justify it, and many such projects never reach production. Good candidates are problems where you can measure before and after, already hold deep data, or currently burn a lot of manual effort. He says AI needs more product management before engineering.
- **Case: a construction equipment company.** Low-resolution cameras in the wrong places could not identify equipment. The real problem was making equipment readiness more efficient, which needed a new camera system. Lesson: do not solve an easy problem that does not fit the business problem, and run at least one cycle on real data to test viability.
- **People and process:** Salespeople entering bad CRM data wreck the forecast. Data governance is where companies struggle most.
- **Three levels of data maturity:** (1) spreadsheets and manual reports, where he says most companies still are; (2) warehouses or lakes with visual analytics; (3) predictive models on top.

## Nathan Christensen, Mitratech and formerly Mineral

*Co-founder and former CEO of Mineral (an HR compliance platform; he describes about 300 employees and more than a million customers at the sale); now on boards of private-equity-backed software companies and writing a book titled The Next Great Company. The module lists him at Mitratech [4][5].*

**Success story: services to software [4].**
- Mineral began as HR experts answering phone calls (the "ER for HR"). It moved to self-service software with experts reserved for hard questions.
- Drivers: scale without constant hiring, anticipated competition, changing customer habits, and better margins from software.
- HR professionals feared being marginalized. Leaders reframed it around the mission and a mantra: "let computers do computer work and people do people work." About three quarters of questions ended up handled by software.
- The executive team also had doubts. They used "disagree and commit": debate openly, then move together.

**Stumble: the AI rollout [4].**
- Early messages stressed AI risks and what not to do, while also urging disruption. Employees froze. A survey showed people knew about AI but rarely used it, because they were afraid.
- The reset lasted months: new AI values, fewer approval steps, and a message of trust and psychological safety. Innovation appeared in Slack channels. He says the Mineral leaders later led the enterprise AI effort at the acquirer.

**Ideas to reuse [4]**
- **Culture eats strategy for breakfast** (a Peter Drucker line he cites). Build identity around the mission (the why), not the how, so people can accept new ways of working.
- **Top-down and bottom-up:** The CEO's conviction matters, but ideas and ownership must come from teams. Executive-only plans cap thinking and create no ownership.
- **Leaders learn in public:** He took AI workshops and used AI personally, and says it is more effective to lead openly without all the answers.
- **"No organization can change faster than the speed of trust."** People need to know a mistake will not be punished.
- **Betty Crocker cake mix:** The mix sold poorly until the recipe asked buyers to crack their own egg. Lesson: give people a part of the change to own.
- **Champions emerge:** Do not preselect them. Put the change out and empower who rises; they were often not the technologists.
- **Fuel versus friction:** Leaders add fuel (incentives, "AI-first" mandates). Often cheaper and more effective is removing friction, as with the US Army's conversations between recruits and their parents (his story).
- **Anticipatory vision:** Organizations hold on to a view of the world slightly too long, like visual persistence; the best look ahead.
- **De-risking:** Start where stakes are low, set guardrails, keep open feedback loops, and admit your own mistakes.
- **Perform and transform:** Run both at once, with KPIs for perform and OKRs for transform, and roughly one third to two thirds of time split (varies by company).
- **Measuring transformation:** Track adoption, hours saved and profitability, and paint a clear picture of success; it is never really "done."

## How the pieces connect

- **Sklarew and Christensen agree:** Readiness, data and people decide outcomes. Sklarew's equipment case and Christensen's stalled AI rollout both show a technically plausible effort failing for non-technical reasons.
- **Lecture versus guest:** The lecture says the CEO mandate comes first [1]; Christensen says it must also be bottom-up [4]. This is the Siebel versus Christensen debate to prepare for.
- **For Discussion Post #3:** The Mineral stories already contain the required pieces (change, drivers, outcome, what he would do differently), so use them as a model, not as your content. Your own example should come from firsthand experience.
- **For your project interviews:** Christensen's Betty Crocker and Army stories are customer-insight examples: the real barrier was a feeling or a conversation, not price or features [4].
